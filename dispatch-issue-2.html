The Dispatch · Issue #2

The one-person agent shop

Friday, October 16, 2026

Chris Abraham's morning starts with a sentence he types to a program: bb good morning.

What comes back is his day, assembled: his calendar, his deadlines, the tasks that slipped, the email that actually came from humans, and the fresh work posted overnight on Upwork — already read, already ranked, in a verdict vocabulary he taught it. At night he types bb good night, and the program writes the day's recap and appends it to a diary that, at this point, writes itself.

The program is called Blackbox — bb — and Abraham built it himself: roughly 12,000 lines of Python, 139 automated tests, running on a DigitalOcean droplet that costs him $6 a month. Understanding why a man would build his own memory tells you most of what this issue is about.

The man who outsourced his memory

Abraham is an SEO and reputation consultant, and he is unusually candid about the hardware he runs on: he has ADHD, aphantasia, and SDAM — severely deficient autobiographical memory. In his own words, his autobiographical memory is "mostly hearsay." The past, for him, doesn't replay; it has to be looked up. So he built the thing that looks it up. bb is not a productivity toy. It is a prosthetic memory with a command line.

Which makes its most important design decision the one that sounds like a limitation: bb can draft, but it cannot act. Every key that spends money or speaks for him stays behind his own yes. It drafts cover letters for the jobs it finds and ranks — and never submits one. In thirty days, 33 proposals moved through that pipeline; one of them became a $12,000 engagement. And the running cost of the prosthetic itself, for the month: $5.33 in AI spend, across 313 requests. Six dollars of server, five dollars of brain, one twelve-thousand-dollar client, and a diary that remembers a life its owner mostly hears about secondhand. (chrisabraham.substack.com)

Last issue was launch season — three companies, seven weeks, one idea. This is the week after launch season, and the most interesting agents in it had no press events. They had a person, a problem shaped exactly like that person, and a small bill.

Laid off, then staffed up — with 27 agents

Linara Bozieva spent eleven years inside eBay's analytics organization. In 2024, she was laid off. What she did next is the post-layoff story of the agent era: she launched Ravenopous, a growth-marketing agency whose staff list reads — Linara Bozieva, plus 27 custom AI agents.

The agency is organized like the companies she used to analyze: three layers — directive, orchestration, execution — with the agents doing the executing across five clients. Her oversight runs about two hours per client per week; her AI spend stays under $1,000 a month. And she is the first to state the catch, which is the part of this story that matters: it works because she knows the domain cold. She described the system in plain language, had the AI generate the agent files, and refined from there — but the architecture itself demands the expertise her eleven years built. The pattern, and it kept repeating all week: agents compress execution time. They do not compress the expertise required to aim them. A layoff took her job. It did not take her judgment, and judgment — aimed well — turned out to be a whole company's worth of leverage. (newclawtimes.com, via Business Insider)

The solo founder who hired a human

Priscila Spezamiglio built Poema Songs, a platform that sells personalized songs, entirely by herself. She has never hired an employee. AI is both her product — it generates the songs her customers buy — and, effectively, her team. By every rule of the hype, her social-media videos should be AI-generated too.

They're made by a freelancer. She tried the AI route first and counted what it actually cost: attempt after attempt to get one usable video, until the hours she was spending exceeded what the human charges. The subscription wasn't the expense; her own time was. So the most AI-native founder in this issue looked at the one job where the machine kept failing her, and hired a person — and that decision, made on receipts rather than religion, might be the most grown-up technology story of the week. (inc.com)

One man, one brand, no staff

Mark Gillilan's Kyoto Botanicals has no full-time employees either — strategy, sourcing, ecommerce, wholesale, marketing, customer relations, and creative all route through one founder and his AI tooling. His signature win wasn't a product launch; it was teaching himself SEO through AI experimentation in about three months, well enough to, in his words, "run circles around larger brands" on visibility. Six months in: brand rebuilt, retail distribution expanded, on what he describes as a meager monthly AI subscription. No drama, no layoff, no twist — just a small brand quietly out-executing bigger ones. (inc.com)

Meanwhile, at scale

The giants had a week too — it's just that their numbers describe a different sport. Google used "Gemini at Work 2026" to unveil a universal enterprise agent and persistent coworker agents carrying their own Workspace identity, email, and audit trail, with early adopters like On, Shopify, and PayPal. BNP Paribas is rolling its assistant out to more than 65,000 employees; SOMPO runs over 10,000 custom agents. Sierra, meanwhile, published the draft of its Personal Agent Protocol — an OAuth-based standard for how personal agents identify themselves to businesses — and after OpenAI skipped the announcement, the October 9 draft (nicknamed Poppy) arrived with 35 more design partners, OpenAI among them: the standards fight is joined, not boycotted. And scale found open source too: Nous raised $90M at a $1.5B valuation for Hermes, now at 24 million clones, while China Telecom's TeleAgent passed 1.5 million registered users — up from 27,000 in mid-June — with a skill marketplace of 50,000 skills, 99% of them built by end users themselves.

The thread through all of it: the big rollouts keep coming — 65,000 employees here, 10,000 agents there — but the leverage per human is highest where the human count is one. A memory for a man whose own is hearsay. An agency out of a layoff. A founder wise enough to hire a human. A brand with nobody on staff and everybody outranked. One person, a few dollars, a machine that moves the work forward.
